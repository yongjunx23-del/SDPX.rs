\begin{abstract}
We describe SDPX, a conic interior-point solver written in Rust. It is derived
from Clarabel.rs and uses one homogeneous self-dual (HSD) predictor--corrector
engine, generic over the scalar type, for both IEEE binary64 and fixed-precision
MPFR arithmetic from 128 to 2048 bits. Its main target is the high-precision
semidefinite programs (SDPs) that arise in the numerical conformal bootstrap.
These problems are polynomial matrix programs whose positive-semidefinite
blocks SDPX keeps in the sampled, factored form introduced by SDPB. The
arithmetic layer stores MPFR values with owned limbs and rounds common
products and sums inline. Every dot product is computed exactly and rounded
once. Large dense products, including congruences $A^\top \operatorname{diag}(d) A$,
use a residue-number-system method on binary64 BLAS and stay correctly rounded,
so the working precision is never lowered. The linear-algebra layer condenses
PSD blocks into a Schur complement and factors it with arrow-structured,
supernodal multifrontal or sparse $LDL^\top$ kernels built on the same exact
products. Iterative refinement runs against the unshifted operator. Every
result is reported in original coordinates and can be audited there. On
matched resampled Ising bootstrap inputs at 768 and 1024 bits on one 64-core
node, SDPX was 4.5--5.7$\times$ faster than SDPB~3.1.0, and all of its points
passed an independent original-coordinate audit. Beyond PSD blocks, SDPX
supports the operator relative entropy cone of quantum information, used for
thermal bootstrap problems, with a barrier evaluated in the generalized
eigenbasis of its two matrix arguments. For bootstrap workflows it adds an
SDPB-style dual feasibility jump that certifies excluded points, SDPB-layout
outputs, and extremal-functional spectrum extraction. In binary64 it does not yet match
MOSEK on most of a 58-problem audited benchmark. We list the known failures
and open limitations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The numerical conformal bootstrap turns consistency conditions on a conformal
field theory into positivity constraints on polynomial matrices
\cite{Rattazzi2008,PolandRychkovVichi2019}. After truncation, each bound or
feasibility test becomes a polynomial matrix program (PMP). The PMP is
converted to an SDP by sampling every polynomial matrix at a set of points and
writing it in a bilinear basis. These SDPs have two unusual properties. First,
the data and the optimal points span many orders of magnitude. Resolving them
requires hundreds of bits of working precision, and the solutions of interest
are reported to tolerances such as $10^{-30}$ or $10^{-42}$. Second, the PSD
blocks have a fixed internal structure (a basis of sampled polynomials times
sample weights) that a generic sparse SDP format destroys.

SDPB \cite{SimmonsDuffin2015,LandrySimmonsDuffin2019} is the standard solver
for this class. It uses arbitrary-precision arithmetic and an
infeasible-start primal--dual method specialized to the sampled block
structure, and it scales across MPI ranks and nodes.

SDPX takes a different set of design decisions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{One engine for every precision.} The interior-point method is
    a homogeneous self-dual embedding \cite{YeToddMizuno1994} taken from
    Clarabel \cite{GoulartChen2024}. It is generic over the scalar, so the
    same code solves binary64 problems (compared against MOSEK) and MPFR
    problems (compared against SDPB).
  \item \emph{A general conic interface.} Besides PSD blocks, SDPX supports
    zero, nonnegative, second-order, exponential, power and generalized power
    cones, and the quantum-information operator relative entropy cone
    together with its limit cone (Section~\ref{sec:ore}). Sampled bootstrap
    blocks are one kind of input, not a separate solver.
  \item \emph{Exactly rounded linear algebra at fixed precision.} Dot products
    and dense matrix products at MPFR precision are computed exactly and
    rounded once. This makes the results independent of thread count and
    summation order, and lets iterative refinement keep working on badly
    scaled systems without lowering the precision.
  \item \emph{Original-coordinate outputs and audits.} Solutions and residuals
    are reported for the problem as given. Sampled factors define the
    operator throughout, and every reported benchmark point is audited
    against the original data by separate code.
\end{itemize}

SDPX provides a native Rust API, a command-line program (\code{sdpx}), a C ABI,
and a PMP-to-SDP converter (\code{sdpx-pmp2sdp}) adapted from SDPB~3.1.0's
\code{pmp2sdp}. The solver core and HSD embedding are adapted from
Clarabel.rs. The solver, arithmetic and FFI crates are Apache-2.0 licensed and
the converter crate is MIT licensed.

\section{Problem class and formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

\subsection{Conic form}

SDPX solves
\begin{equation}
  \min_{x\in\R^n}\; \tfrac12 x^\top P x + q^\top x
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad Ax + s = b,\quad s \in \K,
  \label{eq:primal}
\end{equation}
where $P \succeq 0$ and $\K$ is a product of the cones listed in
Section~\ref{sec:intro}. A PSD cone of order $m$ is stored as $\svec$
coordinates: the upper triangle in column order, with off-diagonal entries
scaled by $\sqrt2$, so that $\langle X, Y\rangle = \svec(X)^\top \svec(Y)$.
The dual is
\begin{equation}
  \max_{x,z}\; -\tfrac12 x^\top P x - b^\top z
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad Px + A^\top z + q = 0,\quad z\in\K^*.
\end{equation}

\subsection{The operator relative entropy cone}\label{sec:ore}

Thermal bootstrap problems for matrix models and lattice systems need
inequalities between the density matrix and a reference state that are not
semidefinite constraints. They are expressed through the operator relative
entropy cone of quantum information,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{ORE}} = \operatorname{cl}\bigl\{(T,X,Y) :
  X, Y \succ 0,\; T \succeq D(X,Y)\bigr\},\qquad
  D(X,Y) = -X^{1/2}\log\!\bigl(X^{-1/2} Y X^{-1/2}\bigr)X^{1/2},
\end{equation}
for $n\times n$ symmetric $T,X,Y$ stacked in $\svec$ form. $D$ is the operator
perspective of $-\log$ (picos' \code{oprelentr}, QICS'
\code{OpPerspecEpi(n,"log")} \cite{HeSaundersonFawziQICS}). SDPX uses the
self-concordant barrier
$f = -\log\det(T-D) - \log\det X - \log\det Y$ with $\nu = 3n$. Only the
primal barrier is known, so it also defines the scaling: $H_s =
(\mu\nabla^2 f(s))^{-1}$, and the centrality condition $z = -\mu\nabla f(s)$
is linearized into the combined step.

All derivatives are evaluated in the generalized eigenbasis of $(X,Y)$:
with $X = LL^\top$ and $L^{-1}YL^{-\top} = U\Lambda U^\top$, the derivative
of $D$ acts entrywise (divided differences of $\log$ on $\Lambda$). The
Hessian factors as $\nabla^2 f = \mathcal{L}^\top
\operatorname{diag}(W\otimes W, S_0)\mathcal{L}$ with a unit block-triangular
$\mathcal{L}$, $W = (Q^\top(T-D)Q)^{-1}$ and $S_0$ the Schur block of
$(X,Y)$ of order $n(n+1)$. The inverse Hessian therefore needs only $S_0^{-1}$
and the explicit $\hat Z\otimes \hat Z$, which avoids squaring the
conditioning of $W$; in the KKT system it appears as $N D N^\top$, and the
augmented and retained-row paths transform right-hand sides by $N^{-1}$.
Dual iterates are kept in $\K^*$ by a Dikin-ellipsoid test at the trial
primal point, $\langle z,s\rangle^2 > (\nu-1)\langle z,(\nabla^2f)^{-1}z\rangle$,
first through an $O(n^3)$ upper bound that drops $S_0$'s curvature (exact on
the central path) and only for inconclusive trials through a conjugate
gradient solve with $S_0$.

Some thermal problems have optimal points at infinity along a recession ray
$(0,W,W)$ of the cone. SDPX also provides the closure of the cone plus a
rank-$m$ ray in reduced coordinates $(T,B,A,C)$,
$T \succeq \bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0 & B\\ B^\top & A-C\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
+ E\,N(A,C)\,E^\top$ with $E = [B; A-C]$ and
$N(A,C) = \int_0^1 (1-s)\bigl((1-s)C+sA\bigr)^{-1}ds$, which shares the
eigenbasis, the barrier form ($\nu = n+2k$, $k=n-m$) and all KKT rules.
Both cones run at every precision, inside the arrow-structured factorization
of Section~\ref{sec:linalg} when many cones couple through a border.
Thermal matrix-model inputs (two\_L4 at 53, 128 and 256 bits; two\_L6 at 53
and 128 bits) are solved and audited, and on a Hubbard thermal campaign the
arrow kernels cut time by 39\% and peak memory by 31\% at 32 threads
(\code{all3}, 2026-11-04). The BFSS thermal problems remain open
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Homogeneous self-dual embedding}

Following Clarabel \cite{GoulartChen2024}, SDPX iterates on
$(x, z, s, \tau, \kappa)$ with $(s,z)\in\K\times\K^*$ and $\tau,\kappa \ge 0$,
and drives the residuals
\begin{align}
  r_x &= Px + A^\top z + q\tau, \\
  r_z &= Ax + s - b\tau, \\
  r_\tau &= \kappa + q^\top x + b^\top z + x^\top P x/\tau
\end{align}
to zero along a central path. A solution with $\tau > 0$ yields
$(x,s,z)/\tau$. A limit with $\tau \to 0$ and $\kappa > 0$ yields a
certificate of primal or dual infeasibility. Each iteration (i) evaluates
residuals and the convergence and infeasibility tests, (ii) computes
Nesterov--Todd (NT) scalings for the symmetric cones
\cite{NesterovTodd1997,NesterovTodd1998}, (iii) updates and factors the KKT
operator, (iv) solves for the affine and combined predictor--corrector
directions \cite{Mehrotra1992}, and (v) chooses a step length and updates the
iterate. The previously accepted iterate is kept so it can be recovered if a
later step fails.

The stopping rules, reduced (``almost'') tolerances, infeasibility detection,
regularization with escalation and the distinct \code{AlmostSolved} status are
Clarabel's. In binary64 the default tolerances are Clarabel's ($10^{-8}$ for
gaps and feasibility). In MPFR the defaults are $\sqrt{\eps}$ for the strict
tests and $\eps^{1/4}$ for the reduced tests, where $\eps$ is the unit
roundoff of the working precision. The bootstrap benchmarks below set explicit
tolerances (e.g.\ $10^{-42}$).

\subsection{Sampled bilinear-basis PSD blocks}
\label{sec:sampled}

A bootstrap PMP block is a polynomial matrix of size $d\times d$ that must be
positive semidefinite on a half-line. After sampling, it becomes a PSD
constraint of side $d\cdot h$, where $h$ is the number of basis rows. SDPX
represents such a block by a basis matrix $Q\in\R^{h\times K}$, whose columns
$q_k$ are the bilinear basis evaluated at the sample points, and by weights.
The primitive columns of the operator are indexed by $(s, r, k)$ with
$r\le s<d$ and $k<K$:
\begin{equation}
  A_{(s,r,k)} \;=\; w_{(s,r,k)}\,
  \svec\!\Bigl(\operatorname{sym}(e_r e_s^\top) \otimes q_k q_k^\top\Bigr),
  \label{eq:sampled-col}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{sym}(M) = (M+M^\top)/2$. The factors $(Q, w)$ define the
operator. A materialized coefficient matrix is used only as a rounded view.
Presolve may remove other columns and rows, remapping factor offsets and the
linear operator together. Chordal decomposition is disabled for sampled inputs
because it would replace the factors by rounded coefficients.

The converter reads SDPB JSON or XML PMPs. Blocks given by explicit sample
points and scalings without a prefactor (as written by PyCFTBoot
\cite{Behan2017} or SDPB.m) are resampled by default: their prefactor is
recovered, it must reproduce every given scaling, and the block is
regenerated with SDPB~3.1's sample points and bilinear bases.
Sample points are found by safeguarded Newton iteration followed by bisection
at full working precision, so generated coefficients can differ from
upstream's in the last digits. On these inputs resampling changes SDPX's
iteration counts noticeably, as shown in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Preprocessing and original coordinates}

Ordinary direct solves apply presolve, chordal decomposition
\cite{VandenbergheAndersen2015} and Ruiz equilibration \cite{Ruiz2001} by
default. Presolve removes infinite orthant bounds and exactly dependent
equalities, merges second-order-cone tail coordinates with zero rows of $A$,
and fixes variables whose only nonzero is a single-entry orthant or equality
row. Postsolve lifts $x$, $s$, $z$ and certificates back. Solutions and
residuals are always reported in original coordinates.

An optional original-coordinate acceptance test (\code{tol\_original}) requires
the returned point to satisfy, at a given tolerance $t$:
$\|b-Ax-s\|_\infty$, $\operatorname{dist}_\K(s)$ and
$\operatorname{dist}_\K(b-Ax)$ at most $t(1+\|b\|_\infty)$;
$\|Px+A^\top z+q\|_\infty$ and $\operatorname{dist}_{\K^*}(z)$ at most
$t(1+\|q\|_\infty)$; and a duality gap of at most $t$ absolute or
$t(1+|\text{primal objective}|)$ relative. The default is $10^{-6}$ in binary64
and off in MPFR. This test exists because the internal Clarabel normalization
divides by $\|x\|+\|s\|$ (or $\|x\|+\|z\|$) of the transformed problem. Presolve,
chordal overlap variables and large solutions inflate those norms. For
sampled problems the test applies the factor-defined operator.

Independently of the solver, the benchmark points in
Section~\ref{sec:results} are checked by separate audit scripts written in
Julia and Python. These scripts recompute the primal and dual residuals,
the duality gap, the PSD conditions and the link between each sampled PSD
matrix and its linear form, in the original coordinates and in high precision.

\section{Arithmetic}
\label{sec:arith}

\subsection{Fixed-precision values with owned storage}

The scalar \code{MpFloat<N>} stores an $N$-limb (64-bit limbs) mantissa, a
kind tag and an exponent inline. It holds no pointers, so copies, moves and
vector reallocations keep ownership independent, and arrays of values are
contiguous. The precision is $64N$ bits (e.g.\ 768 bits is $N=12$). MPFR
\cite{Fousse2007} descriptors are created only for the duration of a call. All
arithmetic rounds to nearest, ties to even.

For regular operands, multiplication up to 1280 bits and addition and
subtraction up to 512 bits are implemented inline and round to nearest-even.
128- and 256-bit fused multiply--add and two-product FMMA use exact stack
kernels with a single rounding. Division at 128--1024 bits is a port of GMP's
schoolbook division with a 3-by-2 inverse, rounded once. FMA at 512--1024
bits keeps GMP's product in place and adds the shifted addend before one
rounding. Every other case calls MPFR. In all cases the result is
bit-for-bit equal to the MPFR result.

\subsection{Exactly rounded dot products}

Every finite value is $M\cdot 2^{e-p}$ with an $N$-limb integer mantissa $M$.
A product of two values is therefore the exact $2N$-limb integer $M_a M_b$
scaled by $2^{e_a+e_b-2p}$. The \code{exactdot} kernel aligns all products
of a dot product to the smallest scale and adds their 64-bit limbs into
128-bit slots, one accumulator per sign. Carries are resolved once at the end.
The result is the exact sum, which a single \code{mpfr\_set\_z\_2exp} rounds
once to nearest-even. The accumulator is bounded at 512 limbs per sign,
about $3\times10^4$ bits of exponent spread. Wider spreads use exact product
descriptors and exact MPFR summation, without allocating the exponent gap.
Non-finite operands fall back to the caller's ordinary chain.

Products use an inlined schoolbook kernel for small widths, a row-product
kernel on aarch64 up to 12 limbs, and GMP's \code{mpn} routines otherwise.
On x86, BMI2/ADX kernels are selected at runtime. Because the sum is exact
before rounding, the result does not depend on term order, partitioning or
thread count, and it is never less accurate than the FMA chain it replaces.

SDPX uses exact dots wherever a long accumulation would otherwise round
repeatedly. Examples are every row of the MPFR refinement residual, sparse
products $Ax$ and $A^\top x$ with enough entries per output, MPFR Cholesky
(\code{xpotrf}) and lower triangular solves (\code{xtrsm}), where each entry
is one exact dot of the completed entries, supernodal and arrow
forward/backward solves, and Schur complement assembly.

\subsection{Residue-number-system exact matrix products}
\label{sec:rns}

For large dense products SDPX follows the approach of SDPB's
\code{bigint\_syrk}, which is itself modeled on FLINT's \code{mul\_blas}. The
steps are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Each operand is aligned to one binary exponent, either per matrix or
    per output window when that needs fewer primes. Every entry then becomes
    an exact integer. Writing $p$ for the precision, $\delta_a,\delta_b$ for
    the exponent spreads of the two operands and $k$ for the inner
    dimension, an output entry is bounded by
    $|S| \le k\, 2^{p+\delta_a}\, 2^{p+\delta_b}$.
  \item Primes are chosen so that their product $M$ exceeds $4|S|$, i.e.\
    with about $2p + \delta_a + \delta_b + \log_2 k + 3$ bits in total. Each
    prime has $b$ bits, where $b$ is chosen such that
    $k\cdot 2^{2b-2} < 2^{53}$. A per-prime product of symmetric residues is
    then a binary64 \code{dgemm} whose every partial sum is an integer
    below $2^{53}$, so it is exact.
  \item Residue encoding and CRT reconstruction are themselves posed as
    \code{dgemm}s, with entries split into short bit chunks multiplied
    against tables of $2^{cj} \bmod p_i$ (encode) or chunks of $M/p_i$
    (decode). Every binary64 product and sum in them is also an integer
    below $2^{53}$.
  \item The CRT reconstructs the exact integer dot product, and each output
    entry is rounded once to nearest-even.
\end{enumerate}
The result therefore equals the correctly rounded exact dot product, which is
exactly what \code{exactdot} returns. If the exponent spread exceeds a fixed
bound the kernel declines and the exact-dot path is used. GEMM, SYRK, upper
triangular products, general congruences, diagonal congruences
$A^\top\operatorname{diag}(d)A$ and the sampled quadratic and bilinear forms
share one streaming, prime-partitioned implementation. Operands that are
constant during a solve (for example the constraint matrix in a congruence)
keep their residues in a cache. At top level the work is split across primes
(encode and product) and across outputs (decode). Calls that already run
inside pool tasks stream primes in small groups with an incremental CRT, so
that each call's working set fits a core's share of cache. Ordinary square
MPFR GEMMs use the residue kernel from 1024 bits at side $\ge 12$; the other
products have their own eligibility rules.

\subsection{What this guarantees}

Because both \code{exactdot} and the residue products return the exactly
rounded result, they are a faster way to compute values at the requested
precision, not a lower-precision approximation. SDPX has no mixed-precision
factorization, no low-precision inner solver and no precision-ladder warm
start (Section~\ref{sec:contracts}). The one place where binary64 is used to
\emph{decide} something about MPFR data is the PSD step-length estimate
(Section~\ref{sec:steps}). Step lengths are a free parameter of the method,
and that estimate is either certified or replaced by a full-precision
computation.

\section{Linear algebra}
\label{sec:linalg}

\subsection{Augmented and condensed KKT forms}

The Newton system has the quasi-definite augmented form
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix} P + \delta I & A^\top \\ A & -(H + \delta I) \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} \Delta x \\ \Delta z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} r_1 \\ r_2 \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the block-diagonal NT scaling and $\delta$ an optional static
shift. SDPX can factor this system directly (\emph{augmented}). It can also
eliminate the rows of PSD blocks, nonnegative cones and small second-order
cones (dimension at most 16) into a Schur complement on $x$ and keep the
remaining cone rows (\emph{condensed}). An eliminated SOC block contributes
$B^\top B$ with $B=W^{-1}A_\K$, using the explicit NT factor. The automatic
rule condenses ordinary PSD data when it has at least 256 PSD $\svec$
coordinates and the reduced dimension is at most a quarter of that, comparing
structural storage estimates. For wide types, problems with $P=0$ whose cones
are all PSD or nonnegative are condensed.
% Source note: docs/ARCHITECTURE.md describes only the 256-coordinate rule;
% the P = 0 rule is from JOURNAL 2026-11-07 (prefer_condensed).
The condensed matrix is written into the primal block of the direct solver's
KKT matrix, so only one authoritative sparse matrix exists. Refinement
always measures residuals with the original augmented operator, not with the
regularized Schur matrix, and no equality rows are inverted or removed.

\paragraph{Sampled blocks.}
For a sampled block, $H^{-1}$ acts by congruence with $G = W^{-1}$. Let $R$
denote the NT factor (Section~\ref{sec:nt}) and set
$V = R^{-1}(I_d\otimes Q)$. The Gram matrix $K = V^\top V$ is computed by one
SYRK (exactly rounded at MPFR precision), and it stays in packed upper storage.
For two primitive columns $p, p'$ whose index pairs into the columns of $V$
are $(a,c)$ and $(a',c')$, the Schur entry is
\begin{equation}
  S_{pp'} = w_p\,w_{p'}\,\tfrac12\bigl(K_{aa'}K_{cc'} + K_{ac'}K_{ca'}\bigr),
\end{equation}
up to the $\svec$ normalization. This is the same structure SDPB exploits. The
product $V$ uses the block-diagonal layout of $I_d\otimes Q$, which costs a
factor $d$ less than a dense product. Its residues are reused while the
scaling is unchanged. The adjoint $A^\top H^{-1}b$ is computed as exact
quadratic or symmetric bilinear forms in the columns of $V$, rounded once per
output.

\subsection{Direct factorization backends}

With \code{direct\_solve\_method = auto}, the direct layer first tries the
structural backends and then a general sparse factorization.

\paragraph{Arrow $LDL^\top$.}
When the positive-signed variables split into structurally disconnected
components, the KKT matrix has the arrow form
\begin{equation}
  K = \begin{bmatrix} \operatorname{diag}(H_i) & B \\ B^\top & C \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad H_i \succ 0,\; C \prec 0 .
\end{equation}
Each leaf $H_i$ is factored densely and independently. The border Schur
complement $C - \sum_i B_i^\top H_i^{-1} B_i$ is formed with exact products
and factored densely. Variants eliminate local bound rows, local or shared SOC
leaves, and local unions of orthant and SOC rows. At MPFR precision the
backend also considers the arrow structure of $-K$, in which retained cone
Hessian rows act as leaves and primal variables as the border, and it takes
the eligible form with less estimated dense work. Admission uses structural
estimates (leaf counts, workspace limits of 512~MiB or a multiple of the KKT
storage, border density). A failed arrow factorization falls back to QDLDL at
the same precision.

On the Ising bootstrap problems the condensed system has this arrow
structure. Leaves correspond to sampled blocks and the border to the free
variables; for example, the border is 104 rows for $\Lambda=27$ and 170 rows
for $\Lambda=35$.

\paragraph{Supernodal multifrontal $LDL^\top$.}
For wide types without arrow structure, SDPX uses a multifrontal supernodal
factorization \cite{DuffReid1983}. The ordering is AMD
\cite{AmestoyDavisDuff1996} without dense-row removal. On a dense-ish Schur
complement, QDLDL's dense-row threshold ($15\sqrt n$) treats every row as dense.
For the condensed Schur complement of the SDPLIB problem \code{dE3} (order 6237),
AMD without that removal gives $2.7\times10^9$ multiply--subtracts against
$3.1\times10^{10}$. Supernodes are relaxed with CHOLMOD's limits
\cite{Chen2008CHOLMOD}. Each frontal matrix is factored by the arrow solver's
dense exact kernels, a recursive $LDL^\top$ using exact dots and residue
products. Fronts start as soon as their children finish, and children are
assembled in a fixed order. Every kernel is either exact or runs in a fixed
order, so the factor does not depend on the thread count or the schedule. The
supernodal backend is chosen when its flops per factor entry are at least 40
and four times its flops are at most $n^3/6$. Otherwise a dense leaf is used,
then the supernodal factor if the system is wide, and QDLDL otherwise.
% Source note: docs/ARCHITECTURE.md's backend table does not list the
% supernodal backend; this paragraph follows JOURNAL 2026-11-07 and
% crates/solver/src/solver/kkt/ldl/supernodal.rs.

\paragraph{General sparse.}
QDLDL (from OSQP \cite{Stellato2020}) is used at every precision, with
elimination-tree parallel scheduling, recorded supernode sweeps and replayed
row patterns on refactorization. In binary64 the faer supernodal $LDL^\top$
is used when estimated flops are at least $10^8$ and flops per factor nonzero
are at least 40. A dense positive leading block is factored by a pooled tiled
Cholesky.

\subsection{Regularization and iterative refinement}

At MPFR precision the static shift starts at zero. Only after a failed
factorization is a shift applied: the first retry uses at least
$\eps\cdot\max|\operatorname{diag}(K)|$, followed by Clarabel's $\times100$
escalation. Shifts affect only the factorization. Newton solves are refined
against the true, unshifted operator, and at MPFR precision every refinement
residual row is one exact dot rounded once. Each right-hand side is refined
with its own residual and measured correction gain. A row whose residual has
reached its own rounding floor (for zero-cone rows,
$\eps\,(\sum|A_r||x| + |b_r|)$) counts as converged. Refinement acceptance
tolerances are never relaxed. In binary64, an outer refinement whose
unnormalized residual has grown continues with GMRES-IR.

Two scope choices reduce work without changing tolerances. On the MPFR
sampled path the affine predictor may be left unrefined while the combined
direction is refined as usual. Also, the constant and affine right-hand sides
share one batched solve.

\section{Algorithmic choices}
\label{sec:algo}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Initial point and the $\tau$ chase}{Initial point and the tau chase}}
\label{sec:start}

For symmetric cones the start solves the KKT system with identity scalings and
shifts $(s,z)$ into the cone, as in Clarabel. If that start is rejected, SDPX
uses a unit interior point with $\tau_0 = \min(1, 1/\max_j d_j)$, where $d$
are the column equilibration scales. Below $\tau_0 = 1$ the shifted KKT dual is
kept and only the primal starts at unit slacks. The reason is that on rejected
bootstrap starts $x$ can be degenerate (up to $10^{77}$) while the dual is
already near its final scale.

A recurring failure mode on bootstrap inputs is what the code calls a
\emph{$\tau$ chase}. The scaled iterate $(x,s,z)/\tau$ must grow by many
orders of magnitude to reach the solution, and HSD gets there by shrinking
$\tau$ slowly while the relative gap stalls. For wide types SDPX detects this
on a first attempt and restarts. The rules are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item since the relative gap last improved tenfold, $\mu$ fell by $10^4$
    while $\tau < 10^{-2}\tau_0$; or
  \item over a six-iteration window, $\mu$ fell at least 30-fold while the gap
    fell less than half as many decades, with $\tau<10^{-2}\tau_0$; or
  \item over the same window, $\tau$ fell at least fourfold while the unscaled
    $\|s\|_\infty$ and $\|z\|_\infty$ each stayed within a factor 1.5, so the
    iterate moves outward along a ray.
\end{itemize}
The restart starts at $\tau_0 = \min(\tau, \eps^{1/8})$, and only the side
(primal or dual) that outgrew its start is rescaled. An attempt that chases
again from that scale restarts at $2^{-341}$, clamped to
$[\eps^{1/2},\eps^{1/3}]$. This value was chosen because a restart lands best
near the solution's $\tau$, which on $\Lambda_{35}$ is a property of the
problem rather than of the precision. An accepted KKT start whose
$\|s\|_\infty\|z\|_\infty$ exceeds $10^{15}$ in equilibrated units starts at
the restart scale directly. These rules are heuristics fitted to the measured
traces recorded in the project journal. Section~\ref{sec:limits} discusses
their cost on \code{dE3}.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Fixed-$\tau$ end phase}{Fixed-tau end phase}}

For wide types, once $\tau$ has stayed within 10\% over ten iterates, the
relative gap is at most $10^{-8}$, the feasibility residual fell tenfold and the
last three steps were at least $0.8$, SDPX fixes $\tau$. Later directions then
keep $\Delta\tau=\Delta\kappa=0$, $\mu$ counts only the cones, and the constant
right-hand side is no longer solved. The termination tests are unchanged. The
phase is off in binary64.

\subsection{NT scaling and the MPFR SVD}
\label{sec:nt}

For a PSD block with primal $S$ and dual $Z$, SDPX computes Cholesky factors
$S = L_1L_1^\top$ and $Z = L_2L_2^\top$, takes the SVD
$L_2^\top L_1 = U\Lambda V^\top$, and forms $R = L_1V\Lambda^{-1/2}$ together
with $R^{-1}$, as Clarabel does. At MPFR precision the default SVD is
right-only. Its singular values come from dqds, and its vectors from inverse
iteration. Near convergence, most singular values of a block can lie in one
narrow window (about 148 of 161 on \code{arch0} within $10^{-3}\|T\|$). Each
vector is therefore first computed independently. Each chain of windows is
then orthonormalized by $V \leftarrow V(I - E/2)$, $E = V^\top V - I$
restricted to window pairs, using two exact GEMMs, repeated while
$(k\max|E|)^2>\eps$. If this does not converge the code falls back to the
in-order Gram--Schmidt path. Full SVDs and fallbacks use bidiagonal QR with
rotations logged and replayed row-parallel. The independent first pass gives
the same bits on every scheduling route. Clustering and pivot thresholds use
the unshifted matrix scale.

HKM (the XZ direction used by SDPB \cite{Helmberg1996}) was
evaluated as an alternative. Its cone update is about $6.5\times$ cheaper, but
on \code{ising11} at 512 bits it increased iterations from 52 to 58 and time
from 37.1 to 41.9~s. It was not adopted for the condensed sampled path.

\subsection{Step lengths and the PSD step certificate}
\label{sec:steps}

For a PSD block the maximal step is $\min(-1/\lambda_{\min}(\Delta),
\alpha_{\max})$, where $\Delta$ is the direction in scaled coordinates. At
MPFR precision SDPX first computes $\lambda_{\min}$ from a binary64 copy by
Householder tridiagonalization and Sturm bisection. With the copy's rounding,
the true value lies within $B = 2(n+1)^2\eps_{64}\|\Delta\|_F$. If $B$ is at
most $10^{-10}$ relative to $\max(|\lambda|, 1/\alpha_{\max})$, the estimate is
accepted. Otherwise a full-precision Cholesky of
$I+\alpha_c\Delta$ at $\alpha_c=(1-10^{-10})\min(-1/\lambda,\alpha_{\max})$
(built from exact dots) decides. Success proves that the true bound exceeds
$\alpha_c$. On failure the full-precision eigensolver runs. In either case the
returned step is within $10^{-10}$ relative of the true bound. The step is
then scaled by the step fraction and kept interior.

\subsection{Centrality correctors}

For symmetric-cone problems with orthant or SOC rows, up to two Gondzio
correctors \cite{Gondzio1996} push orthant products, SOC spectral values of
the scaled Jordan product, and $\tau\kappa$ into $[0.1\sigma\mu, 10\sigma\mu]$.
A corrector is kept only when the step grows by 1\%, and correctors are
skipped once $\alpha\ge0.9$. In binary64 PSD blocks also receive a corrector,
and symmetric problems try a quadratic curve on the affine and combined
directions. At MPFR precision a PSD Gondzio corrector saved 12--17\% of
iterations on the Ising problems. Each call costs one combined solve,
however, and the net effect was a slowdown, so it is not used.

\section{Parallelism}
\label{sec:parallel}

\paragraph{Shared-memory.}
Each solver owns one worker pool (\code{max\_threads}). The pool runs cone
blocks, factorization and long vector work, and dominant blocks split their
tiles over the same pool. The pool width is sized from structural work
estimates that depend only on dimensions and precision. For example, a lone
PSD cone above order 64 counts
$\max(\lceil n^2/64^2\rceil, \lceil n_x^3/(3\cdot 64^3)\rceil)$ units, and
multi-cone pure-PSD problems count their exact condensed-assembly terms. The
faer factors and solves run inside the same pool. PSD contribution buffers
are published by disjoint CSC columns, and each destination adds blocks in
their original cone order. Exact MPFR products give the same rounded result
at every thread count. In binary64, factorization backends may change
rounding with the thread count, and the original-coordinate audit remains the
accuracy gate. Scratch is per thread and reused across iterations. On Linux,
worker binding preserves the caller's CPU mask. Multi-rank runs can start
idle-priority keeper threads when the frequency governor follows load.

\paragraph{MPI.}
MPI is loaded at runtime. The ordinary MPI path replicates input data and
shards the expensive work by blocks. With \code{--partitions}, ranks own whole
blocks (owner partitioning) and share the equality Schur complement. Measured
block costs from earlier runs can be fed to the balancer. Both paths plug into
the same HSD loop. Owner sums fold gathered values in rank order, and exact
refinement residual rows are split across ranks by work. Results on the Ising
problems are in Section~\ref{sec:mpi-results}.

\section{Numerical contracts}
\label{sec:contracts}

The project fixes the following rules. Performance changes are accepted only
if they keep them.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Precision is never lowered.} SDPX uses no silent precision
    lowering, no mixed-precision factorization and no precision-ladder warm
    starts. MPFR values own their storage. Correctly rounded exact
    accumulation (residue products, \code{exactdot}) is allowed.
  \item \emph{Clarabel-style convergence and infeasibility detection.}
    Iterative refinement may change scope (for example which right-hand
    sides are refined), but never its acceptance tolerances.
    \code{AlmostSolved} is never promoted to \code{Solved}, and
    the componentwise feasibility tolerance stays off by default.
  \item \emph{Original-coordinate outputs.} Accepted-iterate recovery is kept,
    and sampled factors define their operator; a rounded materialization is
    never swapped in.
  \item \emph{Default preprocessing.} Direct solves default to Ruiz,
    presolve and chordal decomposition. Data updates require presolve and
    chordal decomposition to be off.
  \item \emph{No benchmark-specific branches and no weakened gates.} Known
    failures stay labeled as failures.
\end{enumerate}
Changes to convergence rules, regularization, refinement scope or presolve
tolerances (for example the $\tau$-chase rules of Section~\ref{sec:start})
require measured evidence: full solves that pass the original-coordinate
audit, and a recorded journal entry.

\section{Bootstrap workflow features}\label{sec:workflow}

A bootstrap study runs many related SDPs: feasibility tests over a grid of
gap assumptions, bisections, and optimizations whose functional is
analysed afterwards. SDPX provides these pieces in the solver and in a
separate post-processing program, \code{sdpx-bootstrap}, which reads the
sampled SDP directory and the solver's JSON result. Because each block's
functional is a polynomial matrix of degree one less than its number of
samples, it is evaluated anywhere in the sampled range by barycentric
interpolation through the samples. The original PMP is not needed.

\paragraph{Feasibility runs and the dual jump.}
An excluded point is one for which a functional $y$ exists with
$c - By \succeq 0$ in the SOS sense: there are Gram matrices
$Y_j \succeq 0$ with $\langle Q_p, Y\rangle = c_p - B_p^\top y$ for every
sample column $p$. In the HSD embedding the dual residual decays with the
gap, so the default stopping rule waits until the gap is closed. SDPB stops
earlier because its infeasible-start steps can jump to exact feasibility.
SDPX obtains the same effect with an explicit test. With the setting
\code{find\_dual\_feasible}, once both residuals are below
$\sqrt{\epsilon_{\mathrm{feas}}}$ it keeps the current $y$, adds to each
Gram matrix the smallest Frobenius-norm correction that satisfies every
sampled equality exactly, and factors the corrected matrices by Cholesky at
working precision. Within one polynomial-matrix block and level pair
$(r,s)$ the correction is $\sum_k w_k f\,q_kq_k^\top$ ($f=1$ on the
diagonal, $1/2$ off it), where $w$ solves a sample Gram system of order
equal to the number of samples. Success certifies $c-By$ as a sum of
squares, and the solver returns the new status \code{DualFeasible}. The
same test is available offline as \code{sdpx-bootstrap certify}, which
reports the equality residual before and after the correction and the
smallest Cholesky pivot.

We built a feasibility pair from the $\Lambda_{11}$ Ising input (512 bits,
$\epsilon = 10^{-42}$) by setting the objective to zero and adding a
one-sample block that enforces $b^\top y + c_0 \ge t$ for $t$ just below or
above the optimum. The excluded side was \code{Solved} after 39 iterations
without the test; with it, the solver stops \code{DualFeasible} after 30.
At the stopped point the corrected equality residual is $10^{-154}$ (from
$3\times10^{-20}$) and the smallest relative Cholesky pivot is
$2.5\times10^{-25}$. Iterates before 30 fail the test, so the stop is the
earliest certifiable one. On the allowed side the solver returns the HSD
dual-infeasibility certificate. With the 512-bit default infeasibility and
$\kappa/\tau$ tolerances ($1.2\times10^{-77}$) this took 67 iterations; with
$10^{-30}$, the threshold scale used by SDPB, it took 43. The ordinary
$\Lambda_{11}$ optimization is unchanged by the setting (54 iterations,
same objective). These are fast-profile runs on a laptop with 4 threads;
the gains have not yet been measured on larger inputs.
% TODO(verify): repeat the feasibility pair on Lambda19/27 on the cluster.

\paragraph{SDPB-layout output.}
\code{sdpx-bootstrap sdpb-out} writes \code{out.txt}
(\code{terminateReason}, objectives, gap, errors, runtime), \code{y.txt},
the per-block primal vectors \code{x\_j.txt} and the Gram matrices
\code{X\_matrix\_j\_p.txt} and \code{Y\_matrix\_j\_p.txt}, so that existing
analysis scripts can read SDPX results. SDPX's sampled primal is SDPB's
$x$ and $X$; $y$ is minus the zero-cone part of $z$, and the PSD part of $z$
holds the $Y$ blocks.

\paragraph{Spectrum extraction.}
\code{sdpx-bootstrap spectrum} implements the extremal functional method.
For each block it evaluates $F(x) = c(x) - B(x)^\top y$ (divided by the
sample scalings) on a refined grid over $[0, x_{\max}]$, normalizes the
smallest eigenvalue by the cancellation scale $\|c(x)\| +
\sum_i |y_i|\,\|B_i(x)\|$, refines every local minimum by golden-section
search at working precision, and reports minima below a threshold as zeros
with their null vectors $v$. It then solves the primal equations
$\sum_z \lambda_z\, v_z^\top B(x_z) v_z = b$ in least squares by Householder
QR. On the $\Lambda_{11}$ optimum it finds 11 zeros in 6 of the 11 blocks
with relative eigenvalues between $10^{-46}$ and $10^{-43}$, all weights
positive, and a relative residual of $1.7\times10^{-25}$ in the primal
equations. The result is the same with 16 and 64 grid subdivisions per
sample interval, and the run takes about 1.4~s. Refining every minimum
matters: near a double zero the grid value is $O(h^2)$, far above the
threshold until refined.

\paragraph{Nearby problems.}
\code{sdpx-bootstrap approx-objective} evaluates the Lagrangian
$c_0' + c'^\top x + b'^\top y - y^\top B' x$ of a nearby SDP of the same
structure at a stored solution, the first-order estimate SDPB provides for
navigator and OPE scans. Checkpoints (\code{--checkpoint},
\code{--restart}) give exact continuations and hot starts for nearby inputs
of the same structure; they are not yet available for partitioned MPI
solves.

\section{Limitations and known failures}
\label{sec:limits}

The following are open. They are listed as recorded in the project's plan.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mixed $\Lambda_{27}$ at 1024 bits.} On a frozen 0.9.1 build the
    solver reports \code{Solved} after 42 iterations, but the point fails the
    $10^{-30}$ original-coordinate audit: dual $1.475\times10^{-23}$, primal
    $3.73\times10^{-28}$, PSD link $2.73\times10^{-26}$, componentwise dual
    0.84355. Primal variables reach $5.8\times10^{118}$. The resampled input
    also fails (dual $2.34\times10^{-23}$, 57 iterations). This problem
    (18\,703 variables) is the size of the SDPB benchmarks. It is a
    different input from the 768-bit $\Lambda_{27}$ of
    Tables~\ref{tab:ising-sdpb} and~\ref{tab:ising-old}, which passes its
    audit. A diagnostic run with explicit gates is prepared but has not been
    run.
  \item \textbf{$\Lambda_{35}$ plateau.} The dual has no Slater point, and both
    SDPX and SDPB spend a long stretch of iterations on a gap plateau. SDPX
    now solves the problem with an audited point at 768 and 1024 bits, but
    the plateau still dominates its iteration count. Shortening it remains
    open.
  \item \textbf{Thermal bootstrap (BFSS) problems.} At 128 bits, BFSS L11u
    ends with status \code{InsufficientProgress} after 107 iterations. The primal variables of
    L11 and t5 are not identified along $\operatorname{null}(A)$. At 512 bits
    the first step on t5 puts up to $8.6\times10^{48}$ into $x$, so residuals
    normalized by $\|x\|$ ($3\times10^{-41}$) no longer measure
    feasibility.
  \item \textbf{Single-block SDPs.} \code{arch0}, one PSD block of order
    161, spends 39\% of a local profile in the NT SVD and stops scaling at
    about 8 threads. \code{gpp100} at
    $10^{-30}$ ends \code{AlmostSolved} after 200 iterations, and the gap
    shrinks only about a decade per 10 iterations after iteration 58.
  \item \textbf{Memory.} Per-thread scratch and the transient workspace of
    exact products dominate peak memory on small problems (e.g.\ \code{arch0}
    0.75~GB), and the matched comparison against SDPB at bootstrap scale has
    not been done.
  \item \textbf{Binary64 problems not solved:} \code{hinf3},
    \code{sched\_100\_50\_orig}, \code{csdr3} and \code{f64\_large}. On
    \code{sched\_100\_50\_orig}, an expanded 4741-dimensional SOC reaches
    $\operatorname{cond}(W^2)\approx5\times10^{16}$, and refinement cannot
    repair the auxiliary row. \code{f64\_large} (a large SU(2) SDP) has PSD
    blocks whose kernel is shared by all constraint matrices and $b$; SDPX
    ends \code{AlmostSolved}, and MOSEK's ``optimal'' points fail the
    $10^{-6}$ audit. A Goldstone LP/SOC problem at 256 bits stalls with the
    dual residual at $10^{-8}$ to $10^{-11}$ while the primal residual reaches
    $10^{-29}$. A gravity problem in binary64 was \code{Solved} with
    $r_d = 4.01\times10^{-6}$ above its $2\times10^{-6}$ audit before an
    earlier fix and has not been re-run since.
  \item \textbf{Feasibility runs.} The dual jump of
    Section~\ref{sec:workflow} stops excluded points early, but allowed
    points still need the HSD infeasibility certificate, and both were
    measured on one $\Lambda_{11}$ pair only.
  \item \textbf{Heuristic restarts.} The $\tau$-chase rules are fitted to
    measured traces. On \code{dE3}, a solve without the restart ends
    \code{Solved} after 62 iterations, but its original-coordinate residuals
    are 180--220$\times$ those of the restarted solve. The restarted solve
    takes 80 iterations, 49 of them after the restart. No predictor in the
    KKT start identifies such problems in advance.
  \item \textbf{Thread scaling.} On the Ising problems, plain single-node runs
    gain at most 3\% from 64 to 128 threads. Single-block SDPs stop scaling at 6--8 threads.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

SDPX shows that a single Clarabel-derived HSD engine, generic over the scalar,
can serve both binary64 and high-precision bootstrap SDPs. Exactly rounded dot
products and residue-number-system matrix products provide most of the speed
at fixed precision without lowering it. On matched resampled Ising inputs at
768 and 1024 bits it reached audited solutions 4.5--5.7$\times$ faster than
SDPB~3.1.0 on one node, mainly through fewer iterations. The same engine
handles the operator relative entropy cone for thermal problems, and the
bootstrap tools certify excluded points and extract the spectrum from the
solution. In binary64 it is not yet competitive with MOSEK on most
problems. The open items are memory use, the $\Lambda_{35}$ plateau, the
mixed $\Lambda_{27}$ audit failure, a fresh matched comparison at
current main, checkpoints for partitioned MPI solves, and measuring
feasibility scans at bootstrap scale.
